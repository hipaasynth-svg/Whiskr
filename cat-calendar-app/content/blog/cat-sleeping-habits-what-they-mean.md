If it feels like your cat is asleep every time you look at her, you're
mostly right. Healthy adult cats commonly sleep somewhere around 12 to 16
hours a day, and kittens and older cats often sleep more than that. That
isn't laziness, and in most cases it isn't a sign that anything is wrong.
It's what a small predator's body is built to do.

The useful part is learning what normal looks like for *your* cat, because
a change from her normal is one of the earliest signs that something is
off.

## Built for short bursts, then rest

Cats hunt the way sprinters run: short, intense effort, followed by a lot
of recovery. A free-living cat makes many brief hunting attempts a day,
most of which fail, and spends the time in between conserving energy. An
indoor cat with a full bowl keeps the same wiring. She just has less to
spend it on.

So a cat's sleep comes in many short blocks spread across the day and
night rather than one long stretch. Some of those blocks are a light doze,
ears still turning toward sounds, ready to be up and moving in a second.
Others are deep sleep, where she's harder to rouse and her body goes
loose. Both are normal, and a cat who naps in short shifts all day isn't
sleeping badly.

## Dawn, dusk, and the 4am problem

Cats are most active around dawn and dusk. A 2019 study that tracked
indoor cats around the clock found exactly that shape: two peaks of
activity and eating, one in the morning before sunrise and one in the
evening, with quiet troughs in the middle of the day and the middle of the
night ([Parker et al., *Animal Biotelemetry*, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40317-019-0188-0)).

That early peak is why so many people get woken before their alarm. Your
cat isn't being difficult. Her body thinks the best part of the day is
starting.

The encouraging finding is that cats who live closely with people shift
toward their people's schedule. In one study, indoor cats who spent their
days alongside their owners were more active during the day and lost the
strong night-time rhythm of cats who spent their nights outdoors
([Piccione et al., *Journal of Veterinary Behavior*, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jveb.2012.09.004)).
Her schedule isn't fixed, and you have a real say in it.

What helps, in practice:

- **Play hard in the evening.** A proper wand-toy session before your own
  bedtime uses up the energy she'd otherwise spend at 4am. We covered how
  to play so it actually satisfies her in
  [The Bored Cat Problem](/blog/cat-enrichment-catios-interactive-play).
- **Then feed her.** Eating after a "hunt" is what tips a cat into the
  groom-and-sleep part of her cycle. A meal right before you go to bed
  moves her crash onto your schedule.
- **Don't reward the wake-up call.** If yowling at 5am reliably produces
  breakfast, she has learned something that works. An automatic feeder set
  for the early hours takes you out of the loop.

## Twitching, paddling, and dreaming

Most cat owners have watched a sleeping cat's whiskers flicker, paws
paddle, or tail twitch. That's normal REM sleep, the stage most associated
with dreaming in people.

Cats actually hold a special place in sleep science. Much of what we know
about REM sleep comes from research on cats in the 1960s. Researchers
found that during REM sleep the brain actively switches off the big
muscles, which is why a dreaming cat twitches rather than gets up and
runs. When that switch was disabled in the lab, sleeping cats stalked,
pounced, and groomed while still asleep, acting out what looked a lot like
hunting ([Morrison, *Journal of Sleep Research*, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.1993.tb00052.x)).

So when your cat's paws are going in her sleep, there's a fair chance
she's somewhere chasing something. Let her finish. A cat woken suddenly
from deep sleep can be disoriented for a moment, and a startled swipe is
not a personal insult.

## What the sleeping positions mean

Sleeping positions are fun to read into, and people read a lot into them.
Here's what's reasonably well grounded, and what's mostly guesswork.

- **Curled into a tight ball, tail over her nose.** Mostly about warmth. A
  tight curl keeps body heat in, which is why you'll see more of it in a
  cool room and less of it in summer.
- **Stretched out flat, or on her side.** The reverse: she's warm and
  comfortable and doesn't need to conserve heat.
- **Belly up.** Her belly is the most vulnerable part of her body, so
  showing it while asleep generally means she feels safe where she is.
  It's a good sign about her environment. It is *not* an invitation to rub
  her stomach. Plenty of cats who sleep belly-up will still grab a hand
  that goes for it.
- **The "loaf", with paws tucked under her body.** A relaxed doze that
  keeps her paws warm and leaves her ready to get up quickly. Common, and
  normal.
- **Eyes half open.** Light sleep. She's resting but still keeping half an
  eye on the room.

Beyond warmth and a general sense of safety, be wary of anyone who claims
a sleeping position reveals a cat's personality or deep feelings. The
honest answer is that nobody has measured that.

## Where she sleeps, and why it moves

Cats choose sleep spots for warmth, height, and safety: sunny windowsills,
the top of the fridge, a closet shelf, a laundry pile still warm from the
dryer. Many rotate between spots over days or weeks. That's normal too.
Moving on from a favorite bed isn't a judgment on the bed.

A few things make a home better for sleeping:

- **A warm spot.** Cats seek out heat, and older cats with stiff joints
  especially appreciate it. A
  [self-warming cat bed](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=self+warming+cat+bed),
  which reflects her own body heat back with no cord, is a safe option.
  For an older cat, a low-wattage
  [heated cat bed](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=heated+cat+bed) designed for
  pets (never a human heating pad) can help.
- **Somewhere enclosed.** A covered
  [cat cave bed](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=cat+cave+bed), or simply a
  cardboard box on its side, gives a nervous or multi-cat-household cat a
  place where nothing can come at her from behind.
- **Somewhere high.** A shelf, a cat tree platform, or a
  [window perch](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=cat+window+perch) gives her a
  vantage point. Height feels safe to a cat.

If she sleeps on your bed, you're far from alone. In one U.S. survey, 31%
of women said they let at least one cat sleep in their bed. The same
survey found cats were reported to be about as disruptive to sleep as a
human partner ([Hoffman et al., *Anthrozoös*, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/08927936.2018.1529354)).
If her night shifts are costing you sleep, the evening play-and-feed
routine above usually helps more than closing the door does.

## Sleep changes that are worth a vet visit

The most valuable thing about knowing your cat's normal sleep is noticing
when it changes. Cats are good at hiding pain and illness, and sleep and
activity are often where it shows first. Talk to your vet if you see:

- **Sudden extra sleep** or reluctance to get up, especially alongside
  eating less, hiding, or not grooming. Lethargy is one of the most common
  ways illness shows up in cats.
- **Less sleep and more restlessness**, particularly in an older cat who
  is suddenly hungry all the time and losing weight. Hyperthyroidism can
  look exactly like this.
- **New night-time waking and crying in an older cat.** The 2021 AAFP
  Feline Senior Care Guidelines list sensory decline (failing hearing or
  vision), cognitive dysfunction, pain, hyperthyroidism, and high blood
  pressure as possible causes of night vocalization. They also note that
  over a quarter of cats aged 11 and older show behavior changes like
  these ([Ray et al., *Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery*, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/1098612X211021538)).
  Several of those causes are treatable, so it's worth a checkup rather
  than putting it down to old age.
- **Changes in how she lies down or gets up**, like hesitating before
  jumping to her usual spot or choosing to sleep somewhere lower. That's
  often joint pain, which is common in older cats and very manageable.
- **Labored or noisy breathing while she's asleep**, or breathing that's
  fast at rest. Get that looked at promptly.

## The short version

A cat who sleeps most of the day, wakes you at dawn, and twitches in her
sleep is a normal cat. Give her warm, safe, high places to sleep, play
with her and feed her in the evening if you'd like your mornings back,
and pay attention when her sleep changes, especially as she gets older.
You know her normal better than anyone.
